    How it works

    Each lane is an independent learner: its own 7-body physics simulation, its own little neural network, its own optimiser. They start from random weights and share nothing but the CPU.

    • Simulation. A planar rigid-body engine written from scratch: capsules, revolute joints with torque-limited motors and hard stops, Coulomb friction against the ground. It runs 5 physics substeps per control step at 60 Hz.
    • Parts. One trunk, two legs (thigh, shin, foot) → 6 actuated joints. The policy outputs a target angle for each joint; the motor does the rest.
    • Observations (23). Trunk lean and speed, hip height, all six joint angles and speeds, two foot-contact flags, and a gait-phase clock.
    • Reward (per second). Forward speed, plus a flight-phase bonus for running with both feet off the ground, minus lean, squatting, effort and jerk; falling costs a flat penalty and ends the episode.
    • Learning. PPO with GAE, 64×64 tanh trunk, gradient norms clipped to 0.5. The backward pass is written by hand and gradient-checked in the test suite.

    The robot on screen is the lane's current policy — watch mode replays it at human speed while the learner behind it keeps churning through episodes.

    Reading the race

    • Amber dots at the feet mark ground contact. A flash of the “FLIGHT” badge means both feet were off the ground at speed — that is running.
    • The first thing every agent learns is to not fall over. Distance stays near zero for a while, then jumps.
    • Agents often find a shuffling gait first, then abandon it for a real stride once shuffling stops paying.
    • Two agents with the same reward will find different solutions. That is why there are lanes.
